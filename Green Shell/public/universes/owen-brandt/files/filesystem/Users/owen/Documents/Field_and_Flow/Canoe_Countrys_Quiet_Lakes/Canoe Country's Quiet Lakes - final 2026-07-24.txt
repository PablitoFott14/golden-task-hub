Slug: QUIET LAKES
Head: Canoe Country's Quiet Lakes
Dek: The Boundary Waters is the most visited wilderness in the country. Its quiet is still out there, one long portage past where most people stop.
By Owen Brandt
Filed July 24, 2026
About 2,200 words, plus If You Go box

The loon called before it was light enough to see the water.

I was already awake, lying on a sleeping pad that had been losing air since midnight and listening to the dew tick on the tent fly. The call came from the middle of the lake: the long, wavering tremolo that sounds like laughter to some people and like grief to others. To me it has always sounded like a question. A second loon answered from somewhere past the point. Then nothing. No motor, no voices, no paddle knocking against a gunwale. Only the small slap of water on the granite below camp and, a while later, a white-throated sparrow working through its four clear notes.

I have paddled the Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness for most of my adult life, and mornings like that one are the reason I keep going back. They are also the hardest thing in canoe country to find. The quiet is still there. It just isn't where most of us go looking for it.

* * *

Start with the numbers, because they explain the problem.

The Boundary Waters covers about 1.1 million acres of northeastern Minnesota, with more than 1,100 lakes and roughly 1,200 miles of canoe routes. In a typical year, around 150,000 people visit, which is why it is often described as the most visited wilderness in the United States.

That description is a little misleading. Spread evenly across a million acres, 150,000 people would almost never see one another. But people don't spread evenly. They come in through a few dozen entry points, most of them at the ends of roads out of Ely, Tofte and the Gunflint Trail, and a great many of them stop at the first good campsite they find.

The Forest Service manages the crowding with a quota system. From May 1 through Sept. 30, each entry point gets a set number of overnight permits a day, and once those are gone, they're gone. The quota keeps the first lake from turning into a parking lot. It does not keep the first lake quiet.

On a Saturday in July, the busiest entry lakes off the Fernberg Road can feel like a marina. Aluminum canoes ride in on outfitters' trailers. Tow boats ferry paddlers up the lakes at the edge of the wilderness where motors are still allowed. Groups of nine, the legal limit, spread their gear across the landing while somebody jogs back to the car for the bug dope.

None of this is bad. Many of those people are on their first trip, and some of them will spend the rest of their lives coming back. I was one of them once, a long time ago, in a borrowed canoe with a map I didn't know how to read.

But if what you want is the loon before daylight, you have to keep going.

* * *

Ask anyone who has spent real time in canoe country how to find quiet, and you will hear some version of the same answer: portages.

A portage is the trail between two lakes, and canoe country still measures them in rods. A rod is 16.5 feet, about the length of a canoe, which made it a practical unit for people who had to carry one. A 40-rod portage is a short walk. A 240-rod portage is three-quarters of a mile with a canoe on your shoulders and a pack on your back, and if the trail crosses a bog, it is three-quarters of a mile of mud that tries to keep your boots.

Every portage works like a filter. Some parties turn around at the first long one. Some never try it. Some camp on the near side and make day trips. Whatever the reason, the lake beyond gets fewer visitors, and the lake beyond that gets fewer still. By the third or fourth long carry, you can paddle a whole afternoon without seeing another canoe, even in the middle of summer.

The Forest Service says as much, more politely, in its trip-planning advice: visitors who want solitude should look at routes with longer portages and consider traveling outside the peak weeks. I'd put it more bluntly. The first lake belongs to everybody. The fourth lake belongs to whoever was willing to get muddy.

I remember the first time I understood that. My wife, Ingrid, and I had carried a long, wet portage in two trips, because we hadn't yet learned how to pack, and we came out of the trees at the far end sweating, bitten and not speaking to each other. The lake in front of us was small and round and ringed with black spruce, with one pine-topped island in the middle. There was no campsite on it that we could see and no other canoe, nothing on the water but a merganser and her ducklings. We sat on the packs and didn't say anything for a long time. Then Ingrid said, "Okay. That was worth it." We have been looking for lakes like that one ever since.

* * *

The filter isn't only about distance. It is also about the calendar.

Most visitors come in July and August, and many of them start their trips on a Friday or a Saturday. Shift either of those and the woods change. A party that goes in on a Tuesday in the middle of September will find open campsites on lakes that were full over the Fourth of July, along with cold nights, almost no bugs and the first yellow in the birches. The quota season ends Sept. 30. In October, the woods belong mostly to grouse hunters and the people who know about October.

Spring can be quiet too, with a catch. In a year like this one, when the ice went out early across much of northern Minnesota, the season opened sooner than usual, and the paddlers who were ready for it had some lakes nearly to themselves. But May water is cold enough to kill you if you end up in it, and the blackflies of early June are their own kind of filter.

Then there is the filter nobody chooses, which is wind. On the big lakes, where waves can build across miles of open water, a breezy day turns rough by late morning, and a loaded canoe in three-foot waves is no place to be. Paddlers who travel early, on the water by 6 a.m. and in camp by noon, see the lakes at their stillest and emptiest. The people who sleep in see the whitecaps, and each other.

* * *

The last filter is the one I think about most: how we behave once we get there.

Quiet in the Boundary Waters is protected to a degree most visitors never notice. Motors are banned on most of its lakes. Since 1949, an airspace reservation has kept most aircraft from flying below 4,000 feet above sea level. Groups are capped at nine people and four watercraft, and camping is allowed only at designated sites. Those rules were written by people who understood that solitude is a resource, as real as clean water or old white pine, and that it can be used up.

None of it was an accident. In the 1940s, floatplanes were flying anglers into resorts on remote lakes, and conservationists, many of them from Ely, pushed for the airspace ban that President Harry Truman signed in 1949. The federal government bought out the resorts in the years that followed. In 1978, after fights that split towns and sometimes families, Congress passed the Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness Act, which ended logging, phased out most motor use and gave the place roughly the shape it has today. Every one of those fights was, in part, a fight about sound.

The rules can't do the whole job, though. A single campsite with a portable speaker can fill a small lake with sound. Voices carry a remarkable distance over still water, especially at dusk, when the wind drops and the air over the lake cools. I once listened to a card game from half a mile away, nearly word for word, and I'm sure somebody on another lake has listened to me explain, at length and with feeling, why the tarp should have gone up before the rain.

Ingrid, who has shared a canoe with me for nearly 30 summers, has one rule for camp: talk as if the people on the next lake can hear you, because they can. It is the best piece of wilderness etiquette I know, and it costs nothing.

* * *

So how do you find a quiet lake? I can tell you how I do it, with one condition up front. I won't name mine. Nobody who has one names theirs, and the reason isn't stinginess. A lake that gets written up stops being the lake that was written about.

Here is what I can tell you.

Choose an entry point for its second or third lake, not its first. Look at the map for the longest portage you are honestly willing to carry, and plan to camp past it.

Learn to read the contour lines, too. A portage that climbs 100 feet over a ridge is harder than a longer one that runs flat along a creek, and the hard ones do the most filtering.

Go midweek, and go late in the season. A Tuesday entry in September will do more for your solitude than any secret route.

Pack light enough to single-portage, which means getting everything across in one trip. It is the difference between walking a 200-rod portage once and walking it three times.

Look for lakes that have no designated campsites and visit them as day trips. Because nobody can camp there, almost nobody goes. Some of the quietest water in the wilderness sits at the end of a short, ugly portage off a main route. Paddle in for an afternoon, eat lunch on a rock, and leave nothing behind.

Talk to the outfitters, and pay attention to what they don't say. A good one will steer you away from the crowds without drawing you a map.

And when you find your lake, keep your voice down.

* * *

The case against all of this is a fair one, and I hear it every time I write about canoe country. The Boundary Waters is public land. It belongs to the kid from Minneapolis on her first trip as much as it belongs to the old hand with a Kevlar canoe and strong opinions about portage yokes. Telling people to go deeper can sound like telling them they're doing it wrong.

That isn't what I mean. The first lake is a gift. It's where people learn to read the wind, rig a tarp and paddle a straight line. Some of my favorite memories are on busy water. Our daughter, Maren, at 9, insisted on paddling bow across a crowded entry lake and argued with the wind the whole way. Our son, Toby, at 6, caught his first walleye within sight of the landing and wouldn't let anyone else touch it, clean it or eat it.

The busy lakes do work the quiet ones can't. They are where a kid learns that a loon is not a duck, where a nervous first-timer finds out that a thunderstorm can be survived in a tent, and where somebody's grandmother paddles one more trip without having to carry anything far.

Still, I think the quiet is worth explaining, because the people who would love it most are often the ones who don't know where to start. Solitude isn't handed out at the landing with the permit. You walk into it, one portage at a time.

* * *

On the last morning of a trip last September, I woke before the loons for once.

The lake was flat and gray. Overnight a beaver had worked the shoreline and left a fan of fresh-cut alder at the edge of our site, like a receipt. Ingrid was still asleep. I made coffee on the stove, carried it down to the rock at the water's edge and sat there long enough to watch the mist lift off the far shore.

Somewhere behind me a woodpecker started up. A pair of loons drifted past the point, diving and surfacing, and neither of them called. They didn't need to. There was no one else there to answer.

We packed up an hour later and carried over the long portage toward the entry lake. By noon we could hear the first outboard on one of the motor lakes outside the wilderness boundary. It wasn't a bad sound. It meant we were almost home. But I paddled the last mile slowly, the way you walk out of a church after everyone else has gone.

IF YOU GO

Permits: Overnight trips from May 1 through Sept. 30 need a quota permit for a specific entry point and start date, reserved through Recreation.gov. The busiest entry points fill early, so book as soon as you know your dates. Day trips without motors need a free day-use permit, filled out at the entry point.

Group size: No more than nine people and four watercraft together, on the water or in camp.

Camping: Only at designated campsites, each with a steel fire grate and a latrine. Lakes without sites are for day visits.

When to go: July and August are the busiest months, and Friday and Saturday the busiest start days. Mid-September brings open sites, cool nights and few bugs.

Gear: A light canoe and a packing list that lets you single-portage will take you farther than any shortcut. Outfitters in Ely, Tofte and along the Gunflint Trail rent both.

Leave No Trace: Pack out what you pack in, hang or barrel your food, and keep your voices low after dark. Sound is part of the place.
